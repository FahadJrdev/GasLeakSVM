In plain words — what is being tested

Questions about your own project check one thing: do you understand it, or did you only complete it? Someone who completed it lists steps. Someone who understands it says why each step was needed.

  • Short answer first, then pause. Let them ask the next question.
  • Use numbers. "98.23% on 960 unseen readings" beats "very accurate".
  • Mention limits yourself. Saying "the gases were deodorant and incense, not LPG" before they do makes everything else you say more believable.

Opening questions

P01

Tell me about your project.

I built a real-time gas classifier: a Support Vector Machine reads seven cheap gas sensors and decides whether the air holds no gas, perfume, smoke or a mixture — 98.23% accurate in about a millisecond per reading.

Then stop. If they want more: I derived the SVM mathematically, tuned an RBF kernel by cross-validation, measured speed, and tested it on later readings, which showed sensor drift is the main limit.

openingsummary
P02

Why is this problem important?

Gas leaks in factories, cold stores and depots kill people, and the cheap alarms most sites use cannot tell a dangerous gas from a harmless smell, so they give false alarms and get ignored.

Examples: Bhopal 1984; ammonia leaks at cold stores in Shanghai (2013) and Alabama (2010); in Bangladesh the BM Container Depot fire (2022, 51 dead) and the Siddique Bazar explosion (2023, 26 dead).

motivation
P03

Your title says "logistics and manufacturing". Where is that in your data?

That is the application setting; the data are a public laboratory benchmark I used to develop and test the method.

The method — a sensor array plus an SVM — is exactly what such sites would deploy. The dataset uses deodorant and incense smoke, so I state clearly that real industrial gases are future work. See page 11.

topic
P04

What is new in your work?

A complete derivation of the multi-class SVM for this problem, a properly tuned SVM that raises the best sensor-only SVM on this data from 88.36% to 98.23%, measured real-time latency, and an honest drift test.

No earlier study on this dataset reported prediction speed or tested on time-ordered data.

novelty

Data

P05

Describe your dataset.

MultimodalGasData: 6,400 readings from seven MQ metal-oxide sensors, recorded every 2 seconds, in four balanced classes of 1,600 — NoGas, Perfume, Smoke and Mixture.

Values are raw 10-bit ADC counts (0–1023), not ppm. It is public on Mendeley Data under CC BY 4.0. I used only the sensor part, not the thermal images.

data
P06

Why seven sensors and not one?

Each MOS sensor reacts to many gases, so one sensor cannot name a gas, but the pattern across seven different sensors works like a fingerprint.

datasensors
P07

How did you split the data?

Stratified random split, 70% training (4,480), 15% validation (960), 15% test (960), seed 42, keeping all four classes equal in every part.

I also ran a second, tougher split in time order (first 70% of each class to train, last 30% to test).

datasplit
P08

What did you learn before training any model?

Smoke and Mixture are clearly different, NoGas and Perfume are almost identical, and readings drift slowly over time.

All three show up later: Smoke and Mixture are 100% correct, the only errors are NoGas↔Perfume, and the time-ordered test reveals drift.

Method

P09

Why did you choose SVM?

It rests on a clean convex optimisation with one global optimum, works well with a few thousand samples, predicts fast because only support vectors matter, and every step can be derived and explained.

That suits a mathematics project and a safety system, where you must be able to justify the model.

methodsvm
P10

Why start with a linear SVM on raw data?

To have an honest baseline, so the improvement from scaling and the kernel can be measured rather than claimed.

Baseline 80.31% → tuned linear on scaled data 84.79% → RBF 98.23%. Each step's contribution is visible.

methodbaseline
P11

Why standardise the features?

The RBF kernel uses distances, and MQ8 varies three times more than MQ6, so without scaling MQ8 would dominate.

Means and standard deviations come from the training data only, so no test information leaks in.

methodscaling
P12

How did you choose C and γ?

Grid search with stratified 5-fold cross-validation on the training set: 42 pairs, 210 fits; the best was C = 100, γ = 10 at 97.63%.

methodtuning
P13

The SVM is a two-class method. How do you get four classes?

One-vs-one: six binary SVMs, one for each pair of classes, and the class with the most votes wins.

methodmulticlass

Results

P14

What are your main results?

98.23% test accuracy, macro F1 0.9823, macro AUC 0.9986, 99.03% leak sensitivity, and about 1.1 ms per prediction.

results
P15

Where does the model make mistakes, and why?

Only between NoGas and Perfume — 17 of 960 test readings — because the deodorant vapour is weak and the sensors respond to it almost as to clean air.

Their class means differ by only a few ADC counts on every sensor. Hazardous classes (Smoke, Mixture) had zero errors.

resultserrors
P16

How do you know it is real-time?

I timed 500 single-reading predictions: mean 1.10 ms, worst 2.88 ms, while a new reading arrives every 2,000 ms.

That uses about 0.06% of the time budget. Even a microcontroller 100 times slower would be fast enough.

resultsrealtime
P17

How does it compare with earlier work?

It beats the earlier sensor-only SVM (88.36%), the LSTM (82%) and the deep sequence models (~93%), and is close to the best random forest (98.67%) — with a single, explainable model.

resultscomparison
P18

What happened in the time-ordered test?

Accuracy fell to 54.37%, because later readings had drifted into regions the model associated with Mixture — this is sensor drift, and it is the main limitation.

It means a real system needs periodic recalibration or retraining. Retraining an SVM on a few thousand readings takes seconds.

resultsdrift

Closing

P19

What are the limitations?

Laboratory gases rather than industrial ones, sensor drift, no calibration to ppm, and latency measured on a laptop rather than a microcontroller.

limitations
P20

What would you do next?

Collect data with real industrial gases over months, add drift compensation, use support vector regression to estimate concentration, and run the model on an ESP32.

future
P21

What did you learn from this project?

That preprocessing and tuning matter as much as the model, and that a strong result must be tested in a harder, more realistic way before you trust it.
