Slides 1–3 · The problem and the aim

S01

Summarise your project in one minute.

I trained a Support Vector Machine that reads seven low-cost MQ gas sensors and says whether the air is clean or holds perfume, smoke or a mixture — 98.23% correct on unseen data, in about one millisecond per reading.

Then add: the baseline linear SVM reached 80.31%; scaling plus the RBF kernel, tuned by 5-fold cross-validation, lifted it to 98.23%. I also applied the method to real pipeline leak sounds (88.8% on 12 classes) and showed honestly that sensor drift drops accuracy to 54.4% on later readings.

slide 1summary
S02

Why did you start with the Titas pipeline numbers?

Because they show the problem is real and local: 9,926 leaking spots were found in only 1,682 km surveyed, and leaks killed 34 people in Narayanganj in 2020 and caused the Siddique Bazar explosion in 2023.

The network is old (built from the 1970s for a 30-year life), and odorant alone finds only part of the leaks. That is why an automatic, continuous detector is useful. The sources are cited in Section 1.3 of the report.

slide 2motivation
S03

What is new in your work? SVMs and this dataset already exist.

The new part is not a new algorithm; it is a complete, honest study of one model on this problem: derived from the mathematics, tuned properly, timed for real-time use, transferred to pipeline sound data, and stress-tested on future readings.

Most papers report only one random-split accuracy. I added three things they usually skip: a measured latency (about 1 ms), a time-ordered drift test (54.4%), and a second, real pipeline dataset with Fourier features.

slide 3novelty
S04

Why only one model? Why not compare with a neural network or random forest?

I chose to study one model in depth rather than many models briefly, and the SVM fits this data well: small, tabular, seven features, with a convex training problem and only two hyperparameters.

A neural network needs more data and more tuning and is harder to explain mathematically. With 98.23% already, the remaining errors come from Perfume and NoGas looking almost identical to the sensors, which is a limit of the data more than of the classifier. The kernel comparison on slide 9 is my internal comparison: linear, polynomial and RBF.

Slides 4–5 · Data and method

S05

Where does the data come from, and is it real gas leak data?

It is the public MultimodalGasData set from a lab in Pune, India (CC BY 4.0): real sensor readings, but the sources were deodorant (perfume) and incense sticks (smoke), not pipeline gas.

So the main study shows that the method can tell gases apart from MQ sensor patterns. The pipeline setting is covered by the deployment design (slide 12) and the GPLA-12 case study on real pipe leaks (slide 13). I say this limit openly.

slide 4data
S06

What exactly is one reading?

One reading is a vector of seven numbers, one 10-bit ADC value (0 to 1023) from each MQ sensor, taken every 2 seconds.

The values are raw counts, not ppm. The dataset gives no calibration to concentration, so the model learns patterns, not absolute gas levels.

slide 4data
S07

Why are there thermal images on your slides if you did not use them?

They come with the dataset and show what each class looked like, so I use them only for context; my model uses only the seven sensor values.

Combining images and sensors is possible future work, but we decided to keep one data type and one model so every result is clear.

slide 4thermal
S08

Why do you scale the data with the z-score?

Because the RBF kernel uses distances, and without scaling the sensors with the biggest numbers would dominate the distance.

Each sensor is shifted to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 using the training set's mean and standard deviation only. Using test statistics would leak information. Scaling alone is a big part of the jump from 80.31% to 98.23%.

slide 5preprocessing
S09

Why 70/15/15, and why stratified?

70% gives enough data to train, the two 15% parts give 960 readings each to check the model honestly, and stratifying keeps exactly 25% of each class in every part.

Validation was used to check the tuned model; the test set was used only once, at the end. Hyperparameters were chosen by 5-fold cross-validation inside the training set.

Slides 6–7 · The SVM and its tuning

S10

Explain the margin in simple words.

The margin is the empty band between the two classes around the separating line; the SVM chooses the line that makes this band as wide as possible, because a wide gap tolerates small changes in new readings.

Its width is \(2/\lVert w\rVert\), so maximising it is the same as minimising \(\tfrac12\lVert w\rVert^2\) subject to \(y_i(w\cdot x_i+b)\ge 1\). This is a convex problem, so it has one best solution.

slide 6maths
S11

What are support vectors, and why does your model have 1,304 of them?

Support vectors are the training points on or inside the margin; only they decide the boundary. 1,304 of 4,480 (about 29%) are support vectors, mostly from NoGas and Perfume, which overlap.

Per class: 331 NoGas, 312 Perfume, 241 Smoke, 420 Mixture. Many support vectors mean the classes are close together. A cleanly separable problem would need only a few.

slide 6maths
S12

What do C and gamma do?

C sets how much a training mistake costs (large C means fewer mistakes but a narrower margin), and gamma sets how far one training point's influence reaches in the RBF kernel (large gamma means a more wiggly boundary).

Too small: the boundary is too simple (underfitting). Too large: it follows noise (overfitting). The heatmap on slide 7 shows both ends.

slide 6maths
S13

The SVM is a two-class method. How does it handle four gases?

With one-versus-one: six SVMs are trained, one for each pair of classes, and each new reading gets the class with the most votes.

Four classes give \(4\times3/2 = 6\) pairs. This is scikit-learn's default for SVC.

slide 6multiclass
S14

How did you choose C = 100 and gamma = 10?

By grid search: 6 values of C times 7 values of gamma, 42 pairs, each scored by 5-fold cross-validation on the training data; C = 100 and gamma = 10 had the best average, 97.63%.

The best pair is inside the grid, not on its edge, so the range was wide enough. Several neighbours (for example C = 1000, gamma = 1) score almost the same, so the result is stable.

slide 7tuning
S15

The animation shows only two dimensions. Is that your real model?

No; the animation uses the first two principal components only so the boundary can be drawn, while the real model uses all seven scaled sensors.

The first two components keep 87.8% of the variance, which is why the picture looks similar to the real problem. On two components the accuracy is lower than the full model.

Slides 8–10 · The results

S16

Why did the baseline do so badly on NoGas and Perfume?

Because perfume (deodorant vapour) barely changes the sensor readings, so NoGas and Perfume overlap, and a straight line on unscaled data cannot separate them.

Their sensor means are almost identical, for example MQ3 about 529 versus 528. Scaling and the curved RBF boundary cut these errors from 189 to 17.

slide 8results
S17

Training accuracy is almost 100%. Is the model overfitting?

No, because validation (98.13%) and test (98.23%) accuracy are almost as high; overfitting would show a large gap between training and unseen data.

The learning curve on slide 9 shows the same: cross-validation accuracy rises steadily toward the training accuracy as data grows.

slide 8overfitting
S18

Why is RBF better than the polynomial kernel?

Both were tuned the same way, and on the test set RBF reached 98.23% against 95.52% for polynomial and 84.79% for linear; the RBF boundary can wrap tightly around each gas cluster.

The RBF kernel also has only two parameters to tune and is numerically more stable than high-degree polynomials.

slide 9kernels
S19

Why report macro F1 and ROC-AUC, not just accuracy?

Accuracy hides which class fails; macro F1 averages precision and recall over the four classes equally, and ROC-AUC shows how well the model ranks each class at every threshold.

Here the classes are balanced, so accuracy and macro F1 agree (98.23% and 0.982). Macro ROC-AUC is 0.9986.

slide 10metrics
S20

For safety, which matters more: missed leaks or false alarms?

Missed leaks, because a missed leak can kill while a false alarm costs time; the model misses only 7 of 720 gas readings (99.0% caught) and gives 10 false alarms out of 240 clean-air readings (4.2%).

If needed, the decision threshold could be moved to catch even more leaks at the cost of a few more false alarms.

Slides 11–13 · Real time and the pipeline

S21

How did you measure real time, and on what hardware?

I timed the trained model predicting one reading at a time, many times, on an ordinary 4-core laptop: mean 1.10 ms, 95% within 1.57 ms, slowest 2.88 ms.

A new reading arrives every 2,000 ms, so the model is about 2,000 times faster than needed. A microcontroller would be slower than a laptop but still far inside 2 seconds. The animation on slide 11 is a replay of recorded readings, not a live sensor.

slide 11real-time
S22

Have you actually installed this on a pipeline?

No; slide 12 is a deployment design showing where the nodes would go, and installing it at a real site is the first item of future work.

The design puts gas-sensor nodes where gas meets people (risers, factory gas lines) and acoustic nodes on the mains, each sending only its decision to a gateway.

slide 12deployment
S23

What did the Fourier transform add in the pipeline case study?

Raw sound samples change randomly with time, but the energy in each frequency band is stable for a given leak, so the DFT turns each 5-second recording into 39 meaningful features.

With raw samples a linear SVM reached 52.9% on 12 classes; with the Fourier features it reached 89.3%, and the tuned RBF-SVM 88.8%. So for sound, the features matter more than the kernel.

slide 13pipeline
S24

GPLA-12 has no "no leak" class and uses air. So what does it prove?

It proves the same SVM method can read real leak sounds from a real pipe rig and tell how strong the leak is (pipe pressure, 88.35%), not that it can detect a leak from nothing.

A field dataset with true no-leak recordings, ideally on natural gas lines, is listed as future work.

Slides 14–16 · Drift, conclusions and the future

S25

Accuracy falls to 54.4% on later data. Doesn't that make your 98.23% meaningless?

No; 98.23% shows the gas patterns are learnable, and 54.4% shows the sensors drift over time, so a real system must be recalibrated or retrained regularly. Both are true, and I report both.

The drop is mainly Smoke: 430 of 480 later Smoke readings look like Mixture, because metal-oxide sensors change their baseline as they warm up and age. Testing on future data is the honest way to see this, which is why I ran it.

slide 14drift
S26

How would you fix the drift problem?

By recalibrating the sensors against clean air at regular intervals, retraining the SVM on recent readings, and checking every new model on the latest data in time order.

Online or incremental learning and drift-correction methods from the electronic-nose literature are options. Collecting data over weeks instead of one session would also help.

slide 14driftfuture
S27

If you had six more months, what would you do first?

Put the model on an edge device such as an ESP32 or Raspberry Pi at a real site and collect field data over weeks, including clean air, so I can measure drift and fix it with periodic retraining.

Then combine gas and acoustic nodes in one alarm gateway, as in the slide 12 design.

slide 15future
S28

What is the single most important thing you learned?

That a high accuracy on a random split is not enough: a model for safety must also be fast, explainable and tested on future data, because real sensors change over time.
